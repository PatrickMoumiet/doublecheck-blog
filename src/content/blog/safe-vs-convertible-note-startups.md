*Both a SAFE and a convertible note delay the valuation question. Here is how they differ and what to check before you sign.*

**Key takeaways:**

- Both let an investor fund now and receive equity later.
- A SAFE is generally not a loan, with no interest or maturity date; a note is debt that usually has both.
- Compare the cap, discount, MFN, interest, maturity, and conversion trigger.
- Model dilution across scenarios and consider legal review of financing documents.

Choosing between a SAFE and a note is mostly about the structure around the promise of equity.

![SAFE versus convertible note](/images/safe-vs-convertible-note-startups/side-by-side.webp)


## 1. What both instruments are trying to do

Early-stage startups often raise money before they are ready to set a company valuation. A **SAFE** (simple agreement for future equity) and a **convertible note** both let an investor put money in now and receive equity later, usually when the company raises a priced round.

The difference is the structure around that promise.

![SAFE versus convertible note: a SAFE is generally not structured as a loan and has no interest or maturity date, while a convertible note is debt that usually has interest and a maturity date](/images/safe-vs-convertible-note-startups/side-by-side.webp)

Our earlier guide on [what a SAFE agreement is](/blog/safe-agreement-startup-founders/) covers the basics. This article is about comparing it with a note.

## 2. SAFE: the simple version

A SAFE is an agreement giving an investor the right to receive equity in the future, on specified events. It is generally not structured as a loan, so it typically has **no interest** and **no maturity date**. Standard SAFE forms are published by Y Combinator, though terms in any specific document can differ.

## 3. Convertible note: the debt version

A convertible note is a loan that is expected to convert into equity. Because it is debt, it typically includes **interest** and a **maturity date**, when the note comes due if it has not converted. That creates a repayment question that SAFEs generally avoid, which is why founders should read the maturity terms carefully.

## 4. Six terms to compare

![Six terms to compare: valuation cap, discount, MFN, interest, maturity date, and conversion trigger](/images/safe-vs-convertible-note-startups/terms.webp)

- **Valuation cap:** a ceiling on the valuation used for conversion.
- **Discount:** a reduced price compared with new-round investors.
- **MFN (most favored nation):** the investor may get better terms later offered to others.
- **Interest:** common on notes, generally not on SAFEs.
- **Maturity date:** relevant for notes.
- **Conversion trigger:** the event that converts the instrument, such as a priced equity round.

Read the actual wording in your document. The names are similar across deals, but the details differ.

## 5. Dilution: model it before you sign

Several SAFEs and notes with different caps can stack up. Before you sign another, build a simple spreadsheet showing how your ownership changes at conversion under a few funding scenarios. If the result surprises you, slow down. For how founder stock is structured alongside this, see [co-founder vesting explained](/blog/cofounder-vesting-cliff-explained/).

## FAQ

**What is the difference between a SAFE and a convertible note?**

A SAFE is generally not structured as a loan and typically has no interest or maturity date. A convertible note is debt that usually has both.

**What is a valuation cap?**

A ceiling on the valuation used when a SAFE or note converts into equity.

**Do SAFEs have interest?**

Generally no. Notes commonly do.

Financing documents affect ownership for years, so professional review is usually worth considering, especially for non-standard terms.
