*A comprehensive corporate compliance guide to U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) Criminal Division guidance, the Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs (ECCP), artificial intelligence risk governance, and internal controls as of November 2026. General information, not legal advice.*

**Key takeaways:**

- The DOJ's updated **Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs (ECCP)** instructs federal prosecutors to evaluate how effectively a company governs the risks of artificial intelligence.
- Deputy Attorney General Lisa Monaco announced that the DOJ will actively seek **sentencing enhancements and harsher penalties** for corporate crimes committed or facilitated using AI.
- Federal prosecutors examine whether corporate AI governance is **integrated into overall enterprise risk management** rather than relegated to an isolated IT or engineering silo.
- Companies must establish **concrete technical guardrails and testing protocols** to prevent both internal employee misuse and external manipulation of corporate AI models.
- When companies use AI tools *inside* their compliance programs (e.g., automated transaction monitoring or sanctions screening), prosecutors will demand proof of **accuracy, reliability, and human oversight**.

## 1. The quick answer

When a corporation faces federal criminal scrutiny—whether for securities fraud, Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) violations, healthcare fraud, or antitrust violations—the U.S. Department of Justice evaluates the effectiveness of the company's internal compliance program under its **Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs (ECCP)** guidance.

Following major policy directives from DOJ leadership, the ECCP formally requires federal prosecutors to assess whether a corporation actively manages the risks associated with **artificial intelligence and emerging disruptive technologies**. Corporations can no longer treat generative AI or automated algorithms as casual productivity tools. To earn favorable treatment, avoid indictment, qualify for declinations, and mitigate corporate criminal fines, enterprises must prove they have implemented a well-resourced, tested, and actively monitored AI governance framework.

## 2. The DOJ ECCP revision and the Monaco directive

The integration of artificial intelligence into federal criminal enforcement was catalyzed by Deputy Attorney General (DAG) Lisa Monaco, who issued a clear policy directive: **where AI is deliberately misused to supercharge white-collar crimes, federal prosecutors will pursue stiffer sentencing enhancements**.

In tandem with this warning, DAG Monaco directed the Criminal Division to incorporate emerging technology risk assessments into the ECCP. The updated ECCP addresses three fundamental questions that federal prosecutors must ask about every corporate compliance program:
1. *Is the corporation's compliance program well designed?*
2. *Is the program being applied earnestly and in good faith—meaning, is it adequately resourced and empowered to function effectively?*
3. *Does the corporation's compliance program work in practice?*

The revised guidance weaves artificial intelligence into all three prongs, requiring companies to show that compliance oversight evolves at the same speed as technological deployment.

## 3. Core evaluation criteria for enterprise AI risks

Federal prosecutors evaluating a corporation's AI governance under the ECCP focus on specific operational dimensions:

- **Technology-Specific Risk Assessments:** Prosecutors assess whether the company has conducted formal, documented risk assessments regarding the use of AI tools in its commercial business operations, products, and customer communications.
- **Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) Alignment:** The DOJ looks at whether AI governance is woven into the company's broader ERM structure, or if tech adoption outpaced legal, risk, and audit functions.
- **Continuous Monitoring and Model Auditing:** Static policies are insufficient. Prosecutors examine whether the company continuously tests and validates its models to ensure they remain compliant with criminal and regulatory statutes.
- **Clear Acceptable Use Policies:** Companies must maintain clear, documented guidelines outlining authorized versus prohibited use cases for generative AI, large language models (LLMs), and external automated platforms.

## 4. Guardrails against employee and insider misuse

A primary area of prosecutorial inquiry is whether a corporation has instituted effective technical controls to prevent internal employees, contractors, or executives from misusing AI to commit fraud or conceal misconduct:

### Potential Misuse Scenarios Scrutinized by Prosecutors
- **Falsified Records and Documentation:** Employees using generative AI to produce fabricated vendor invoices, altered audit logs, or synthetic contract approvals to bypass internal payment controls.
- **Intellectual Property and Trade Secret Theft:** Unauthorized pasting of confidential source code, trade secrets, or client non-public data into unsecured, public consumer AI platforms.
- **Market Manipulation and False Disclosures:** Marketing or investor relations teams using unvetted AI tools to draft exaggerated public filings, financial forecasts, or sustainability reports.

To satisfy the ECCP standard, companies must demonstrate **active technical controls**—such as data-loss prevention (DLP) tools, enterprise wrappers with zero data retention, prompt logging, and multi-signature authorization for financial approvals.

## 5. Auditing AI used within the compliance function

The ECCP places equal emphasis on how the compliance department itself utilizes artificial intelligence. In recent years, multinational companies have integrated machine learning into automated sanctions screening, transaction monitoring for money laundering (AML), and whistleblower complaint routing.

Federal prosecutors evaluate whether compliance-embedded AI meets rigorous operational standards:

1. **Reliability and Trustworthiness:** Has the compliance department independently validated that its automated screening models are mathematically accurate, properly calibrated, and not generating catastrophic false negatives?
2. **Access to Quality Data:** Does the compliance AI have timely access to complete, uncorrupted organizational data, or is it hobbled by legacy data silos?
3. **Accountability and Human-in-the-Loop:** Are algorithmic alerts systematically reviewed by qualified compliance personnel, or does the company rely on automated rubber-stamping without human verification?

If a compliance department relies on an automated transaction monitoring tool that fails to catch corrupt foreign payments due to flawed training data or lack of tuning, the company cannot claim its compliance program was effective in practice.

## 6. Practical corporate governance blueprint

Corporations seeking to align their compliance programs with the DOJ ECCP standards must implement a four-pillar operational framework:

1. **Form a Cross-Functional AI Governance Committee:** Establish a formal governance body comprising the Chief Compliance Officer, General Counsel, Chief Information Security Officer (CISO), Chief Technology Officer (CTO), and Head of Internal Audit to oversee AI procurement and risk.
2. **Implement Enterprise-Wide AI Inventory & Risk Tiering:** Maintain an active inventory of every AI application, model, API, and third-party SaaS tool touching company networks, categorizing them by operational and regulatory risk level.
3. **Execute Role-Specific Compliance Training:** Move beyond generic compliance modules. Provide targeted training for software developers, finance teams, and commercial sales representatives on the legal risks and acceptable boundaries of AI tools.
4. **Empower Whistleblower Reporting and Investigation:** Ensure the company's internal reporting hotline is equipped to handle reports of algorithmic manipulation, data poisoning, or AI-enabled fraud, backed by specialized forensic audit capabilities.

## FAQ

**What is the DOJ Evaluation of Corporate Compliance Programs (ECCP)?**

The ECCP is official guidance used by federal prosecutors to evaluate whether a corporation's compliance program was well-designed, adequately resourced, and operating effectively when deciding criminal charges and penalties.

**How did the DOJ update the ECCP regarding artificial intelligence?**

In 2024, the DOJ updated the ECCP to formally instruct prosecutors to evaluate how corporations identify, manage, and mitigate risks associated with artificial intelligence and disruptive technologies.

**Will federal prosecutors seek harsher penalties for crimes involving AI?**

Yes. Deputy Attorney General Lisa Monaco directed federal prosecutors to seek increased sentencing enhancements for white-collar crimes that are deliberately facilitated or accelerated using AI technology.

**Does the ECCP evaluate AI used inside the compliance department itself?**

Yes. Prosecutors must examine whether the AI tools a company uses for compliance monitoring, transaction screening, and fraud detection are reliable, trustworthy, and properly tested.

**What consequences do companies face if their AI compliance program is deemed ineffective?**

An ineffective compliance program eliminates opportunities for criminal declination, increases corporate fines under the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines, and frequently results in mandatory independent compliance monitorships.
