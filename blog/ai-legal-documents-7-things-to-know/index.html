How to Use AI for Legal Documents: 7 Things to Know Before You Draft

AI can draft a contract, NDA or demand letter in seconds, but it cannot promise the result is correct. Check the sources, protect confidential data, pick the right model for the task, and have an attorney review anything that matters. This is general information, not legal advice.

7 things to know when drafting legal documents with AI

1. Verify every citation

AI can invent cases, statutes and quotes that look real. A public tracker of court cases involving AI-fabricated content passed 2,000 by September 2026. Courts have sanctioned lawyers for failing to check, not for using AI. If a document cites a law or case, read the original.

2. Know where your data goes

Consumer chatbots are not built for confidentiality. In February 2026, a New York federal judge ruled that documents drafted with a public AI tool were not protected by attorney-client privilege. Use a paid or enterprise plan with clear data terms, and leave out names, amounts and deal details when you can.

3. Match the model to the task

No single model wins everywhere. Comparisons commonly credit Claude for nuanced drafting and long contracts, ChatGPT for versatile drafting and numbers, and Gemini for very long documents and research with current sources. Rankings shift with every release, so test two models on a sample clause before committing.

4. Give it the context a lawyer would ask for

State the jurisdiction, governing law, parties, purpose and which side you represent. Without that, you get generic boilerplate, and contract law varies by state and country.

5. Check the essentials are there

Parties, definitions, scope, payment, term and termination, liability limits, confidentiality, IP ownership, governing law, dispute resolution and signatures. Ask the AI what is missing, then compare against a trusted template.

6. Read for consistency, not just fluency

AI prose sounds polished even when defined terms drift, clauses contradict each other, or cross-references point to the wrong section. Read the whole draft once, looking only for those problems.

7. Get an attorney to review what matters

AI is a drafting assistant, not a lawyer, and the person who signs carries the risk. Arrive with an organized first draft and your questions: that usually makes attorney time faster and cheaper, not unnecessary.

FAQ: AI and legal documents

Can I use ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini to write a contract?

Yes, as a starting draft. A contract’s validity depends on its terms and the parties’ agreement, not on who or what wrote it. But AI can omit key clauses or misstate the law, so review the draft carefully and have an attorney check anything high-stakes.

Which AI is best for legal documents?

It depends on the task. Published comparisons tend to favor Claude for careful drafting and contract analysis, ChatGPT for general drafting and numerical work, and Gemini for very long documents and research. Purpose-built legal tools add verified citations and stronger security. Test on your own documents.

Can AI hallucinate legal information?

Yes. Every major model can produce fake cases, invented statutes and misquoted rules in confident language. Treat every citation and legal claim as unverified until you have read the source.

Is it safe to put confidential information into an AI tool?

Only with care. Consumer tools may not protect confidentiality, and a 2026 U.S. ruling found documents made with a public AI tool were not privileged. Prefer enterprise plans with written data terms, and remove identifying details where possible.

Is an AI-generated contract legally binding?

It can be, if it meets the usual requirements for a valid contract in your jurisdiction and the parties agree to it. Being AI-written neither helps nor hurts. Errors, missing terms and unenforceable clauses are the real risk.

Do I still need a lawyer if I use AI?

For anything with real money, rights or liability at stake, yes. AI can speed up drafting and help you prepare, but it cannot give advice tailored to your situation or take responsibility for the outcome.
