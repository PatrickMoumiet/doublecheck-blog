Tennessee ELVIS Act: AI Voice Cloning & Likeness Protections

A comprehensive legal compliance guide to the Tennessee Ensuring Likeness, Voice, and Image Security (ELVIS) Act (T.C.A. § 47-25-1101 et seq.), unauthorized generative AI voice cloning, right of publicity protections, and commercial risk management as of November 2026. General information, not legal advice.

Key takeaways:

  • Signed into law as a national first, the Tennessee ELVIS Act explicitly establishes that an individual’s voice is a protected property right, shielding it from unauthorized AI cloning.
  • The statute imposes secondary liability on tech companies, software distributors, and platforms that provide tools whose primary purpose is unauthorized voice or likeness replication.
  • Rights under the Act are fully descendible and assignable, empowering heirs, trusts, and commercial estates to protect deceased artists and personalities.
  • Willful commercial exploitation of an individual’s voice or likeness without consent constitutes an actionable civil tort and a Class A misdemeanor.
  • Commercial brands, creative agencies, and AI developers must implement strict rights clearance workflows and unambiguous contractual licensing before deploying synthetic voice models.

1. The quick answer

On July 1, 2024, the state of Tennessee enacted the Ensuring Likeness, Voice, and Image Security (ELVIS) Act (Public Chapter No. 741), dramatically overhauling the state’s Personal Rights Protection Act of 1984.

The ELVIS Act represents the nation’s first comprehensive state statute explicitly protecting the human voice from unauthorized artificial intelligence reproduction, voice cloning, and synthetic simulation. Designed to protect music artists, voice actors, and everyday citizens from deepfake exploitation, the law creates powerful civil causes of action, establishes secondary liability for developers of cloning software, and enforces criminal misdemeanor penalties against willful commercial infringers.

2. The origin and passage of the ELVIS Act

Tennessee, home to Nashville’s global music recording industry and Memphis’s iconic musical heritage, was uniquely exposed to the sudden emergence of hyper-realistic generative AI voice cloning. In 2023, viral incidents—such as the unauthorized AI-generated track “Heart on My Sleeve” simulating the voices of Drake and The Weeknd—exposed severe gaps in federal intellectual property laws.

Traditional right-of-publicity statutes in most states protected an individual’s name, photograph, and visual likeness, but omitted explicit protection for the acoustic sound of a human voice. Governor Bill Lee and the Tennessee General Assembly passed the ELVIS Act with unanimous, bipartisan legislative support to close this loophole.

3. Expanding the right of publicity to human voice

The core statutory innovation of the ELVIS Act is the explicit codification of voice as a distinct, inviolable property right:

  • Codified Property Right: T.C.A. § 47-25-1103 confirms that every individual possesses a property right in the use of their name, photograph, voice, and likeness in any medium.
  • Expansive Voice Definition: The statute covers not only pre-recorded vocal tracks, but also the live, recognizable vocal characteristics of a performer—including simulated acoustic vocal patterns produced by machine learning algorithms.
  • Unambiguous Commercial Prohibition: Using an individual’s voice or digital replica in advertising, products, merchandise, or commercial endorsements without express prior authorization violates the statute.
  • Posthumous Property Continuation: The property right does not expire upon death. It descends to heirs, executors, or exclusive licensees, continuing for an initial ten-year post-mortem period and persisting indefinitely as long as the estate actively exploits the commercial rights.

4. Secondary liability for AI tool developers

Perhaps the most potent and controversial aspect of the ELVIS Act is its creation of secondary and contributory liability for software creators and service providers.

Under T.C.A. § 47-25-1105(b):

“A person is liable to a civil action under this section if the person distributes, imparts, or otherwise makes available an algorithm, software, tool, or other technology, service, or device, the primary purpose or function of which is the production of an individual’s photograph, voice, or likeness without authorization.”

This provision directly targets tech developers and consumer AI platforms. If a platform is engineered, marketed, or optimized primarily to generate unauthorized voice clones—or fails to implement reasonable guardrails preventing users from replicating real individuals—the platform provider faces joint and several liability alongside the infringing end-user.

5. Statutory remedies and criminal penalties

The ELVIS Act provides severe enforcement teeth against unauthorized commercial exploitation:

  1. Injunctions and Temporary Restraining Orders: Courts are empowered to issue emergency injunctions prohibiting the distribution, streaming, or sale of unauthorized synthetic media.
  2. Actual and Disgorgement Damages: Prevailing plaintiffs can recover actual economic damages sustained, as well as disgorgement of all profits attributable to the unauthorized commercial exploitation.
  3. Impoundment and Destruction Orders: Courts may order the impoundment and permanent digital destruction of all infringing recordings, synthetic materials, and specialized software models.
  4. Criminal Misdemeanor Penalties: Willful and unauthorized commercial use of an individual’s voice or likeness constitutes a Class A misdemeanor, punishable by criminal fines and confinement.
  5. Standing for Commercial Licensees: Crucially, the statute grants legal standing not only to the individual artist, but also to exclusive recording and distribution licensees (such as record labels and publishing companies), ensuring that institutional rights holders can enforce protections directly.

6. Commercial clearance and compliance protocol

Organizations commercializing generative audio, text-to-speech tools, or digital marketing campaigns must execute a rigorous compliance protocol:

  1. Conduct Complete Rights Clearance: Never deploy an AI-generated vocal asset in a commercial advertisement, podcast, or product without obtaining an executed written license from the voice talent or their estate.
  2. Review Contractual Digital Replica Clauses: Ensure talent contracts explicitly delineate whether digital replica rights are granted, specifying authorized platforms, geographic territories, and compensation structures.
  3. Audit Consumer AI Platform Guardrails: Software developers offering voice cloning or audio synthesis must institute technical safeguards that block users from uploading reference audio of known artists without verified identity authentication.
  4. Implement Notice-and-Takedown Infrastructure: Establish dedicated legal mechanisms allowing artists, voice actors, and estates to flag and expeditiously remove unauthorized digital replicas.

FAQ

What is the Tennessee ELVIS Act?

The Ensuring Likeness, Voice, and Image Security (ELVIS) Act is a landmark Tennessee law enacted in 2024 that upgraded the state’s right-of-publicity statutes to explicitly protect an individual’s voice from unauthorized AI cloning.

Does the ELVIS Act only protect famous musicians and celebrities?

No. The ELVIS Act protects every individual residing in Tennessee, establishing that every human being has a property right in their own voice, likeness, and image.

What is secondary liability under the ELVIS Act?

The law establishes civil liability for anyone who produces, distributes, or makes available software, tools, or services whose primary purpose or function is the unauthorized production of an individual’s voice or likeness.

Can an estate enforce voice rights under the ELVIS Act?

Yes. The ELVIS Act confirms that voice and likeness rights are descendible property rights that can be owned, licensed, and legally enforced by heirs, estates, and trusts posthumously.

How does the ELVIS Act interact with the proposed federal NO FAKES Act?

The ELVIS Act served as the blueprint for federal legislation like the NO FAKES Act, which seeks to create a uniform federal intellectual property right protecting digital voice and likeness replicas nationwide.
