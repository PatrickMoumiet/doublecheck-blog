California AB 2602 & AB 1836: AI Digital Replica Contract Rules

A comprehensive legal guide to California Assembly Bills 2602 and 1836, contractual restrictions on generative AI digital replicas, posthumous publicity rights, and entertainment compliance as of November 2026. General information, not legal advice.

Key takeaways:

  • Enacted in late 2024 and taking effect on January 1, 2025, California AB 2602 and AB 1836 establish strict statutory boundaries for creating and commercializing AI digital replicas.
  • AB 2602 renders boilerplate, sweeping AI replica clauses void and unenforceable in contracts for living performers unless the agreement meets strict transparency and legal representation standards.
  • Performer consent to digital replication is legally invalid unless the performer was actively represented by independent legal counsel or a collective bargaining union.
  • AB 1836 outlaws the unauthorized commercial exploitation of deceased performers’ voices and likenesses, requiring advance licensing from their legal estates.
  • Entertainment studios, game developers, ad agencies, and corporate brands face statutory damages, profit disgorgement, and contract invalidation for deploying unvetted AI replicas.

1. The quick answer

In response to historic Hollywood labor strikes and the rapid rise of synthetic media, California enacted two landmark statutes governing generative AI in the entertainment industry: Assembly Bill 2602 and Assembly Bill 1836.

Together, these laws reshape entertainment contracts and right-of-publicity protections. AB 2602 protects living performers by invalidating sweeping, generic contractual clauses that allow studios or employers to create AI digital replicas in lieu of actual human performance. AB 1836 extends rigorous safeguards posthumously, prohibiting media companies from commercially generating digital replicas of deceased personalities without the express prior consent of their estates. Any business contracting with actors, voice talent, influencers, or models in California must completely overhaul standard grant-of-rights provisions to ensure statutory compliance.

2. Legislative background and SAG-AFTRA advocacy

The passage of AB 2602 and AB 1836 was driven by the 2023 SAG-AFTRA and Writers Guild of America strikes, where artificial intelligence emerged as an existential bargaining issue. Performers expressed intense alarm over predatory contract language:

  • Perpetual Digital Grants: Traditional boilerplate clauses frequently granted studios the perpetual right to use a performer’s name, likeness, and performance “throughout the universe in any and all media now known or hereafter devised.”
  • Uncompensated Human Replacement: Studios sought the ability to scan background actors or voice talent for a single day’s wage, subsequently generating endless synthetic performances across multiple film sequels or video games without additional compensation or consent.
  • Deceased Icon Exploitation: Technology enabled the resurrected visual and vocal appearance of deceased actors in major motion pictures without meaningful compensation or artistic control granted to their surviving families.

Championed by SAG-AFTRA and signed into law by Governor Gavin Newsom, AB 2602 and AB 1836 establish permanent statutory floors that cannot be bypassed through private contract adhesive waivers.

3. California AB 2602: Living performer protections

Codified in the California Labor Code, AB 2602 declares that any contractual provision authorizing the creation and commercial use of a digital replica of an individual’s voice or likeness in lieu of the individual’s live performance is contrary to public policy and completely unenforceable unless specific statutory criteria are fulfilled:

The Three Mandatory Enforceability Tests

To be legally enforceable under AB 2602, the contract must satisfy three concurrent conditions:

  1. Reasonably Specific Description: The agreement must contain a detailed, reasonably specific description of the intended commercial use of the digital replica, including the specific audiovisual project, duration, and artistic scope. Catch-all, unlimited waivers are void.
  2. Mandatory Professional Representation: The performer must have been represented by independent legal counsel who specializes in digital replica agreements, or by a certified collective bargaining representative (such as SAG-AFTRA), during the negotiation of that specific clause.
  3. Explicit Performance Substitution Notice: The contract must clearly state that the digital replica will substitute for the performer’s actual live performance.

If an employer or production company inserts an unrepresented digital replica clause into standard employment paperwork, non-disclosure agreements, or independent contractor templates, the entire provision is legally void as a matter of law.

4. California AB 1836: Posthumous estate rights

While AB 2602 protects living talent, AB 1836 amends California Civil Code § 3344.1 (the Astaire Celebrity Image Protection Act) to protect deceased personalities.

Prior to AB 1836, California’s posthumous right-of-publicity law contained broad exemptions for audiovisual works, motion pictures, and musical compositions. Studios leveraged these loopholes to insert hyper-realistic digital avatars of deceased actors into new film projects without obtaining estate consent.

Key Mandates of AB 1836

  • Prior Estate Authorization Required: Any person or commercial entity that produces, distributes, or makes available an audiovisual work or sound recording containing an unauthorized digital replica of a deceased personality is liable for civil damages, unless prior consent was obtained from the personality’s legal estate.
  • Digital Replica Definition: A computer-generated, highly realistic electronic representation that is readily identifiable as the voice or visual likeness of the deceased individual.
  • Descendible Property Right: The consent requirement applies for 70 years following the death of the personality, aligning with standard copyright duration principles.

5. Statutory damages and civil liabilities

Both statutes provide formidable financial remedies to deter unauthorized synthetic replication:

Civil Liabilities Under AB 1836

A party that violates AB 1836 by commercially deploying an unauthorized deceased replica is liable to the estate for:

  • Statutory Damages: An automatic minimum award of $10,000, or the actual economic damages suffered by the estate, whichever is greater.
  • Disgorgement of Profits: All gross profits attributable to the unauthorized use of the digital replica that were not factored into actual damages.
  • Treble Damages and Punitive Awards: Where willful infringement is proven, courts may award punitive damages and reasonable attorney’s fees.

Contract Invalidation Under AB 2602

Under AB 2602, attempting to enforce an invalid digital replica provision exposes employers and production companies to breach-of-contract actions, labor commissioner complaints, and preliminary injunctions halting the release or distribution of the media project.

6. Entertainment and production compliance roadmap

Media producers, commercial advertising agencies, game developers, and corporate marketing departments must execute a comprehensive contract compliance overhaul:

  1. Purge Blanket AI Language: Review all talent releases, voiceover agreements, model releases, and actor contracts to strike out broad, unspecific digital duplication clauses.
  2. Draft Standalone Digital Replica Addenda: When digital replication is legitimately desired, draft a separate, dedicated exhibit detailing the exact scope, technology used, compensation structure, and delivery milestones.
  3. Document Performer Legal Representation: Maintain written certifications and signature blocks confirming that the performer’s licensed attorney or union representative reviewed and negotiated the digital replica exhibit.
  4. Obtain Certified Estate Licenses: Prior to using historical footage, voice samples, or archival imagery to train an AI model or generate a deceased performer’s avatar, execute a formal licensing agreement with the registered estate or trust.

FAQ

What is California AB 2602?

California AB 2602 is a statute that protects living performers by rendering broad, unspecific contract provisions allowing AI digital replicas unenforceable unless the contract contains a reasonably specific description and the performer was represented by legal counsel or a union.

What is California AB 1836?

California AB 1836 is legislation that protects deceased personalities by prohibiting the commercial use of a digital replica in audiovisual works or sound recordings without the express prior consent of the performer’s estate.

Can an employer include an AI digital replica clause in standard onboarding paperwork?

Under AB 2602, broad boilerplate grants in standard employment agreements are completely unenforceable if they allow an AI replica to replace the performer’s live services without independent legal representation and specific project disclosures.

What are the penalties for violating California AB 1836?

A person or entity that produces an unauthorized digital replica of a deceased performer is liable for statutory damages of $10,000 or actual damages, whichever is greater, plus the disgorgement of profits.

Are documentaries and parody protected under AB 1836 and AB 2602?

Yes. Both statutes contain strong First Amendment exemptions protecting news reporting, public affairs, documentary films, historical biographies, parody, and satire, provided they do not mislead audiences into believing the replica gave an authentic live endorsement.
